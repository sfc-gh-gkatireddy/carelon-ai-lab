Overview

5 MIN

Carelon teams spend significant time answering recurring questions about member cost drivers, quality gaps, and care utilization. Answers are often locked inside claims databases and scattered benefit policy documents — requiring manual SQL or analyst requests that take days.

In this lab, you will build a Member Intelligence Agent: an agentic AI experience that lets any team member ask natural language questions and get answers that draw on both structured claims data and unstructured policy documents — all governed, all within Snowflake.

The foundation: a semantic view

The foundation for this is a semantic view — which is really a base ontology for your payer domain. It defines the entities that exist (members, claims, providers), how they relate to one another, and the vocabulary your business actually uses to describe them. Without this layer, an agent is guessing at what your column names mean. With it, the agent reasons over business concepts rather than raw schema.

Use Case Context — Why This Matters for Carelon

This lab is grounded in three of a payer's highest-priority AI patterns:

  • Cost-of-care analytics — which members, providers, and conditions are driving cost?
  • Quality / Stars gap identification — which members are missing preventive services or medication adherence milestones?

The dataset — 100% synthetic

This lab uses a fully synthetic, HIPAA-safe payer dataset modelled on a commercial health plan population. It was purpose-built for this lab — no real member, patient, or PHI data is used at any point. The data mirrors the schemas and business logic that payer data engineering teams work with every day.

STRUCTURED DATA (4 TABLES)

  • Members — 10,000 synthetic health plan enrollees with demographics, plan type, PCP assignment, and chronic condition flags (diabetes, hypertension, COPD, etc.)
  • Medical Claims — ~116,000 claim lines with ICD-10 diagnosis codes, CPT procedure codes, billed/allowed/paid amounts, and claim status
  • Pharmacy Claims — ~54,000 prescription fills covering drug names, NDC codes, days supply, drug class, and paid amounts
  • Providers — 2,000 providers with specialty, NPI, network status, and average cost per visit

UNSTRUCTURED DATA (4 POLICY DOCUMENTS)

  • Formulary Guidelines — drug tiers, prior authorization rules, step therapy requirements, GLP-1 and specialty drug policies
  • Medical Benefits Summary — coverage rules for preventive care, specialist referrals, mental health, physical therapy, and durable medical equipment
  • Quality & Stars Measures — HEDIS measure definitions, Stars thresholds, eligible member criteria for colorectal screening, diabetes care, medication adherence
  • PA Denial Letter — a synthetic prior authorization denial letter used with AI_EXTRACT

Agent Outcome — What Your Agent Will Be Able to Do

By the end of this lab you will have a running Member Intelligence Agent that any team member — analyst, care manager, or operations lead — can query in plain English. The agent will be able to:

  • 📊 Answer structured analytics questions — "Which members with diabetes have the highest pharmacy spend this year?" — answered with a live SQL query over claims data, no analyst required.
  • 📋 Retrieve policy & benefits information — "What are the prior authorization requirements for GLP-1 medications?" — answered from the formulary document, with source cited.
  • 🔍 Identify care gaps & quality opportunities — "Show me members eligible for the colorectal cancer screening Stars measure who haven't been screened" — combining claims data with HEDIS criteria from policy docs.
  • 💡 Surface trends & explain the "why" — "Why did pharmacy costs increase in Q3?" — the agent reasons across multiple data sources to give a synthesized, contextual answer.
  • 🏷️ Classify claims on demand — "Classify an ER visit with diagnosis I21.0" — the agent calls a custom AI_CLASSIFY tool you build to assign clinical risk categories, no rules engine required.
  • 📄 Read documents you hand it — You will use AI_EXTRACT to pull member ID, drug, denial reason, and appeal deadline out of a PA denial letter PDF — turning an unstructured document into a queryable row.
Your agent is your design

In Step 6 you will define its persona, name, tone, guardrails, and focus area — making it specific to how your team would actually want to use it. Two attendees can build very different agents on the same dataset.

Explore the Dataset

Run these five queries to get familiar with the data and to seed query history — Semantic View Autopilot in Step 4 uses your account's query history to suggest better metrics and dimensions, so running these now improves the generated view.

SQLExploratory queries — run as CARELON_LAB_ROLE
USE ROLE CARELON_LAB_ROLE; USE WAREHOUSE CARELON_LAB_WH;
USE DATABASE CARELON_LAB_DB; USE SCHEMA PAYER;

-- 1. Member population by chronic condition
SELECT chronic_condition, COUNT(*) AS member_count
FROM MEMBERS GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 2 DESC;

-- 2. Medical spend by service type
SELECT service_type,
       COUNT(*)                     AS claims,
       ROUND(SUM(paid_amt), 0)      AS total_paid
FROM MEDICAL_CLAIMS GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 3 DESC;

-- 3. Top 10 drugs by total pharmacy spend
SELECT drug_name, drug_class,
       COUNT(*)                     AS fills,
       ROUND(SUM(paid_amt), 0)      AS total_paid
FROM PHARMACY_CLAIMS GROUP BY 1, 2 ORDER BY 4 DESC LIMIT 10;

-- 4. Total medical cost of care by chronic condition (member + claims join)
SELECT m.chronic_condition,
       COUNT(DISTINCT m.member_id)  AS members,
       ROUND(SUM(mc.paid_amt), 0)   AS total_medical_paid
FROM MEMBERS m
JOIN MEDICAL_CLAIMS mc ON m.member_id = mc.member_id
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 3 DESC;

-- 5. Provider utilization and average paid by specialty
SELECT p.specialty, p.network_status,
       COUNT(*)                     AS claims,
       ROUND(AVG(mc.paid_amt), 2)   AS avg_paid_per_claim
FROM MEDICAL_CLAIMS mc
JOIN PROVIDERS p ON mc.provider_id = p.provider_id
GROUP BY 1, 2 ORDER BY 3 DESC LIMIT 10;

These queries touch all four tables and exercise key dimensions and measures — this is exactly the pattern Autopilot looks for when building the semantic view in Step 4.

04

Build Your Semantic Layer — Autopilot & Ontology

15 MIN

Cortex Analyst translates natural language questions into SQL — but it needs a semantic view to understand your data. A semantic view is your domain's base ontology: it declares which entities exist, how they relate to each other, and what vocabulary describes them — mapping business concepts like "total cost of care", "care gap", and "medication adherence" onto the physical tables and columns in your database.

Rather than hand-authoring a YAML file, you will use Semantic View Autopilot — Snowflake's AI-powered feature that inspects your tables and generates a complete, business-ready semantic view automatically.

What is Semantic View Autopilot?

It is an AI assistant built into Snowsight's Analyst interface that reads your table schemas, column names, data samples, and recent query history, then generates a Semantic View — a native Snowflake object stored in your database schema, just like a regular view. It includes dimensions, measures, table relationships, and verified sample queries — with zero manual YAML editing required. You review, refine, and save it directly to CARELON_LAB_DB.PAYER.

What you just built is a base ontology

A semantic view is not only a BI convenience layer — it encodes the structural pieces of a domain ontology: entity classes (members, claims, providers), typed relationships between them with explicit cardinality, a controlled vocabulary via synonyms so enrollee, patient, and member resolve to the same concept, and enumerated taxonomies for fields like chronic condition and service type. It also references external industry ontologies — ICD-10, CPT, NDC, and NPI are all formal classification systems in their own right. This is why your agent can answer questions phrased in language that appears nowhere in your column names.

Where a fuller ontology would go next: the current model has no cross-entity concept unification. MEMBERS.CHRONIC_CONDITION = 'Diabetes' and MEDICAL_CLAIMS.ICD10_CODE = 'E11.9' both mean diabetes, but nothing in the model connects them — so the agent must be told they are the same concept. Adding a condition-to-code crosswalk, a drug class hierarchy (GLP-1 → antidiabetic → endocrine), and inference rules would turn this base ontology into a reasoning layer.

Step 5b — Create the Cortex Search Service

Run the following SQL to create a table from the staged documents and build the search service on it:

SQLCortex Search setup
USE ROLE CARELON_LAB_ROLE;
USE DATABASE CARELON_LAB_DB; USE SCHEMA PAYER;
USE WAREHOUSE CARELON_LAB_WH;

-- Parse staged policy docs into a searchable table (one row per line)
-- pattern skips the PDF: it is binary, and Step 5d reads it with AI_EXTRACT instead
CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE POLICY_DOCUMENTS AS
SELECT
  REPLACE(METADATA$FILENAME, '.txt', '') AS doc_name,
  $1::VARCHAR                             AS content
FROM @POLICY_DOCS (file_format => POLICY_TEXT_FORMAT, pattern => '.*[.]txt')
WHERE $1 IS NOT NULL;

-- Create Cortex Search service over policy content
CREATE OR REPLACE CORTEX SEARCH SERVICE BENEFIT_POLICY_SEARCH
  ON content
  ATTRIBUTES doc_name
  WAREHOUSE = CARELON_LAB_WH
  TARGET_LAG = '1 hour'
  EMBEDDING_MODEL = 'snowflake-arctic-embed-m-v1.5'
AS (
  SELECT doc_name, content FROM POLICY_DOCUMENTS
);
Indexing takes 1–2 minutes

The Cortex Search service will take 1–2 minutes to build its embedding index. You will see it appear in AI & ML → Search once it is ready. You can proceed to Step 6 while it indexes.

Step 5d — Cortex AI Functions: Extract and Classify

Snowflake's Cortex AI functions let you run intelligence directly inside SQL — no ML pipeline, no external API, no data movement. Two functions are especially relevant for payer workflows:

  • AI_EXTRACT reads a document (PDF, image, or text) and pulls out structured fields you specify — member IDs, diagnosis codes, denial reasons, dollar amounts — returning them as JSON. Think of it as turning any unstructured document into a queryable row.
  • AI_CLASSIFY takes a text input and assigns it to one of a set of categories you define. For claims data, this means auto-categorizing service types, flagging high-risk encounters, or triaging appeals — without writing rules or training a model.

You staged a synthetic PA denial letter in Step 5a alongside the policy documents. Open it to see what you're extracting from: view the PDF. It contains a member ID, diagnosis, HbA1c, step therapy status, and a full denial reason — the kind of document a utilization management team processes hundreds of times a day.

Run the following query. It will take 1–3 minutes as Snowflake parses the PDF and extracts the fields. Start it running, then continue to Step 6 — by the time you finish creating your agent, the results will be waiting in your query tab.

SQLAI_EXTRACT from PA denial letter — run as CARELON_LAB_ROLE, continue to Step 6 while it runs
USE ROLE CARELON_LAB_ROLE; USE WAREHOUSE CARELON_LAB_WH;
USE DATABASE CARELON_LAB_DB; USE SCHEMA PAYER;

SELECT AI_EXTRACT(
  TO_FILE('@POLICY_DOCS', 'pa_denial_letter.pdf'),
  ['member_id', 'member_name', 'decision', 'drug_name',
   'icd10_code', 'hba1c', 'denial_reason',
   'appeal_deadline', 'prescriber_name', 'prescriber_npi']
)::VARCHAR AS extracted;

When the query completes, you will see a JSON object with every field extracted — member ID, drug, diagnosis code, HbA1c, the full denial reason, and the appeal deadline — all from a 2-page PDF. One SQL function, zero parsing code.

In Step 6, you will also add AI_CLASSIFY as a tool your agent can use to automatically categorize medical claims into clinical risk categories — giving it the ability to reason beyond just the raw data.

Automate a Recurring Query

Some questions are worth asking every day — cost trends, denial spikes, care gap counts. Instead of remembering to ask your agent each morning, you can turn any CoWork conversation into a scheduled automation that runs on a cadence and delivers the results to you automatically.

Try it now with this example:

  1. Ask your agent: "Show me the top 10 members by total medical spend this month."
  2. Once you get a response, click the Automate button (clock icon) on the response card.
  3. Set the schedule — for example, Daily at 8:00 AM.
  4. Choose where the results should be delivered — CoWork will surface them in your Automations tab in the left nav, and optionally notify you via email.
  5. Click Save.

Every morning at 8 AM, CoWork will re-run the query against live claims data and have the fresh results waiting for you — no SQL, no scheduling infrastructure, no analyst ticket. The automation runs under your credentials, so row-access policies and column masking apply automatically.

Good candidates for automation

Think about the questions your team asks repeatedly: daily denial counts by service type, weekly pharmacy spend by drug class, monthly care gap lists for outreach. Any question your agent can answer today can become a scheduled delivery tomorrow.

What You Built Today

  • A synthetic payer data foundation — four tables covering members, medical claims, pharmacy, and providers
  • A base ontology for the payer domain, expressed as a semantic view (CARELON_LAB_DB.PAYER.MEMBER_INTELLIGENCE) — entity classes, typed relationships, business vocabulary, and enumerated taxonomies, generated and refined with Autopilot
  • A Cortex Search service over benefit policy and formulary documents — enabling RAG over unstructured content
  • Structured extraction from a PDF — AI_EXTRACT pulled member ID, drug, diagnosis code, denial reason, and appeal deadline out of a prior authorization denial letter in a single SQL call
  • A custom AI_CLASSIFY tool (CLASSIFY_CLAIM) that categorizes claims into clinical risk categories — and is wired into the agent, so it can classify on demand mid-conversation
  • A Cortex Agent with your own persona, instructions, and three tools — Analyst, Search, and your custom classifier — built and iterated with CoCo
  • A live Snowflake CoWork session that any business user could access without writing a single line of SQL

From Lab to Production — Where Do You Go From Here?

The pattern you built today is a direct template for production use cases on real payer data platforms. Here are the natural next steps:

Reuse the semantics you already have

Don't rebuild your ontology from scratch. Your business definitions already exist — in Power BI reports, Tableau workbooks, dbt models, and the data dictionaries your team keeps in Excel and Confluence. Semantic View Autopilot ingests Power BI files directly (.pbit or .pbix): DAX measures become metrics, relationships and calculated columns carry over. For the messier sources — spreadsheets, docs, legacy SQL — use CoCo to extract the measures, filters, and business logic. The definitions your business already reconciles against become the ones your agent uses, so agent answers match the reports leadership already reads.

Deepen the ontology

Your base ontology has no cross-entity concept unification yet — CHRONIC_CONDITION = 'Diabetes' and ICD10_CODE = 'E11.9' mean the same thing but nothing links them. Add a condition-to-code crosswalk, a drug class hierarchy (GLP-1 → antidiabetic → endocrine), and clinical grouper logic so the agent can reason across entities instead of being told they match.

Add more tools

Extend the agent with a custom stored procedure tool — e.g. a SEND_CARE_GAP_ALERT() procedure that triggers an action from a natural language instruction.

Govern with Horizon

Apply row-access policies and column masking to the payer tables so the same agent returns only the data each role is authorized to see — critical for PHI in production.

All data in this lab is synthetic and was purpose-built for training. No real member, patient, or PHI data is used at any point.
